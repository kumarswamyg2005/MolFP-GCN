The idea in 30 seconds

Computers cannot read a molecule. We have to turn it into numbers first. The numbers are called a fingerprint.

The old way, ECFP, uses a fixed recipe. The same molecule always gets the same fingerprint, whatever you want to predict.

Our way uses a graph neural network (GCN). It treats the molecule as a drawing: atoms are dots, bonds are lines. It learns its own fingerprint, shaped by the task.

Say this in the vivaWe built a network that learns its own molecular fingerprint, and checked fairly whether it beats the fixed ECFP fingerprint with a random forest.

The two problems we tested

  • ESOL: 1,128 molecules. Predict how well each dissolves in water (a number called logS).
  • Tox21: 7,823 molecules. Predict 12 yes/no toxicity tests for each one.

The model: how the GCN thinks

Your one lineEach atom repeatedly asks its neighbours what they know, and the molecule ends up as 256 learned numbers.

Step 1. Molecule to graph

A text code for the molecule (called SMILES) is read by a chemistry library. Every atom becomes a dot with 35 numbers: which element, how many bonds, charge, hydrogens, shape, whether it is in a ring, and its weight. Every bond becomes two arrows, one each way.

If a code is invalid, our parser returns a friendly reason. It never crashes.

Step 2. Talking to neighbours

Picture people in a room. Each person asks their neighbours what they know, averages it with their own view, and updates. We do this 3 times. After 3 rounds each atom knows about atoms up to 3 bonds away.

The averaging is balanced by how many bonds an atom has, so a busy carbon does not shout louder than a lonely oxygen.

Step 3. One fingerprint per molecule

Atoms have no natural order, so we combine them in an order-free way: the average of all atoms plus the maximum of all atoms. Together that is 256 numbers: the learned fingerprint. A small network on top turns it into the answer. Total size is about 88,000 weights, small enough for a laptop.

Which atoms mattered? (saliency)

We ask: if this atom's numbers changed a little, how much would the answer move? We colour the result. Red pushes the prediction up. Blue pushes it down. White barely matters.

Be honest: saliency shows what the model used, not proven chemistry. The model may have found a shortcut.

Questions you may get

Why 3 layers?

ECFP4 sees 2 bonds away. Three layers see one bond further. We also tried 1, 2 and 4 layers. All scored about the same (see Rakesh's section).

What is the weakness of GCN?

It ignores bond types. A double bond C=O and a single bond C–O look the same to it. We already store bond information, so a future edge-aware layer (GINE, D-MPNN) would only change model.py.

What is a residual connection?

From layer 2 on, a layer adds its result to its input instead of replacing it. This keeps training stable and helps stop every atom looking the same.

Why mean and max both?

Mean says what the molecule is made of overall. Max catches one standout group, like a single nitro group that can decide toxicity.

What is over-smoothing?

With too many rounds, every atom averages in nearly the whole molecule and they all look alike. We did not see it up to 4 layers.

Data, fair splitting and training

Your one lineWe made the test as hard and honest as possible, then trained both models the same way, so the numbers can be trusted.

The data

  • Both datasets come from the MoleculeNet collection. The download script checks a checksum and refuses to continue if a file changed.
  • Tox21 had 7,831 rows. RDKit could not read 8 of them, so we dropped those and logged it. That leaves 7,823.
  • About 17% of Tox21 answers are missing, and only 7.5% of known answers are "toxic".

The scaffold split (the most important idea)

A scaffold is a molecule's core ring skeleton. Many molecules in a dataset are near-twins sharing a scaffold. If one twin is in training and the other in the test, the model can just remember the answer.

So we put every scaffold family entirely in one place: 80% train, 10% validation, 10% test. A test checks that no scaffold appears in two places.

Both models read the same saved split file, and the evaluation code checks they used identical data.

Training, step by step

  1. Settings in a fileLearning rate 0.001, batches of 64, up to 300 rounds. Both datasets use the same settings.
  2. Each round (epoch)The model guesses, we measure the mistake, the optimiser (Adam) nudges the weights.
  3. Check on the validation setThis is a separate set used only to pick the best round.
  4. Early stoppingIf there is no improvement for 30 rounds, stop and keep the best saved model.

Two losses (how the model is told it is wrong)

  • ESOL: mean squared error on the solubility number.
  • Tox21: binary cross-entropy that skips missing answers (never treats them as zero) and gives rare toxic examples more weight.

How we measure

  • RMSE: typical size of the error in solubility units. Lower is better.
  • R²: how much of the variation we explain. 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than guessing the average.
  • ROC-AUC: pick one toxic and one safe molecule at random. How often does the model rank the toxic one higher? 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect.

Why you can trust it (reproducibility)

  • Random seeds 0, 1 and 2 control the split and the model start, and we report mean ± spread.
  • A test scrambles the test molecules and retrains. The trained weights come out identical, which proves the test set is never used in training.
  • Numbers used to scale targets come from the training set only.
  • Every number in the report is filled in automatically from the results files.
  • A fresh clone runs end to end with make all in 11 minutes on a laptop and gives identical results.

Questions you may get

Why is the ESOL result so variable?

ESOL has only about 1,100 molecules, so the test set is small and a few unusual families change the score a lot. That is why we use 3 seeds.

Why not just a random split?

It would be easier and look better, but it leaks near-copies of training molecules into the test. Real use means new chemistry, so scaffold split is the honest choice.

How do you handle missing Tox21 labels?

We mask them out of the loss and the score. We never fill them with zero.

The baseline, the experiments and the demo

Your one lineWe gave the old method a fair fight, tested our design choices one at a time, and built an app so anyone can try it.

The baseline: ECFP4 + random forest

ECFP4: a fixed recipe. For each atom it looks 2 bonds out, turns that neighbourhood into a code, and sets bits in a 2048-slot list. Same molecule, same bits, every time.

Random forest: many decision trees voting. We train one forest for each Tox21 test, using only rows that have an answer for that test.

Why it lost: the fingerprint is fixed and cannot adapt to the task, and hashing can make different substructures collide in the same slot. The GCN can change what it pays attention to.

The demo app

Run make demo and open localhost:8501. Draw a molecule or paste its code. The app predicts solubility and the 12 toxicity scores, and colours atoms by saliency.

Say out loud: this is a research demo. It must not be used for safety or medical decisions.

Limitations (say them first)

  • GCNConv ignores bond types, so double and single bonds look alike.
  • ESOL is small, so results vary a lot from seed to seed.
  • Tox21 probabilities are not calibrated, because rare toxic examples were given more weight.
  • Our Tox21 split is probably easier than the published one, so do not compare with papers directly.

Next steps

  • Use an edge-aware layer (GINE, GATv2 or D-MPNN) so bonds count. This is the top priority.
  • Calibrate the Tox21 probabilities.
  • Test on more datasets and more seeds.

Questions you may get

Is the comparison fair?

Yes. Both models use the same split file, the same seeds, and the same metrics. A check confirms they used identical data.

Did the forest do anything better?

One Tox21 test, SR-ARE. Everywhere else the GCN was ahead.

Can the app be wrong?

Yes. It is a small model trained on about a thousand to eight thousand molecules, and it is least reliable on molecules unlike its training data.

Words in plain English

  • SMILES: a line of text that describes a molecule, like CCO for ethanol.
  • Graph: dots (atoms) joined by lines (bonds).
  • Fingerprint: a list of numbers describing a molecule.
  • GCN: a neural network where each node averages its neighbours' information.
  • Scaffold: the core ring skeleton of a molecule.
  • Seed: a starting number for randomness. Same seed, same result.
  • Epoch: one full pass over the training data.
  • Overfitting: memorising the training data instead of learning the pattern.
  • Ablation: an experiment that changes one part to see if it matters.
